### Structure 1: The Problem-Then-Principle

Use when: you have a concrete problem the reader recognizes, and the value is in articulating why it keeps happening (a principle) rather than just listing fixes.

Shape:
1. Open with the specific problem or scene — make the reader feel it
2. Name what is actually going on beneath the surface (the principle or mechanism)
3. Show what changes when you understand the principle (implications, not just tips)
4. Close with a reframe or a single actionable consequence of the principle

Good for: opinion pieces, analysis, insight-driven posts
Avoid when: the reader needs step-by-step instructions

---

### Structure 2: The Guided How-To

Use when: the reader needs to do something and needs a clear, sequential path through it.

Shape:
1. State what the reader will be able to do by the end and why it matters to them specifically
2. List any prerequisites or decisions they need to make first
3. Walk through the steps in sequence; group related micro-steps under named stages
4. Address the most common failure points or edge cases
5. Close with what to do next or how to know it worked

Good for: tutorials, process guides, reference posts
Avoid when: the topic is primarily conceptual or the outcome is contested

---

### Structure 3: The Comparative Frame

Use when: the reader is choosing between approaches, tools, or ideas and needs a principled way to think about the tradeoffs.

Shape:
1. Set up the decision: what is being compared and why the choice matters
2. Establish the evaluation criteria before presenting the options (this prevents the comparison from feeling rigged)
3. Evaluate each option against the criteria honestly, including where each one wins
4. Give a recommendation that is conditional on reader context, not universal
5. Close with the one thing the reader should figure out about their own situation before deciding

Good for: comparison posts, buying guides, "which approach" analysis
Avoid when: one option is clearly dominant — use a direct recommendation instead

## Angles / hooks

Describe the recurring angles or hook strategies that suit your blog. An angle is a lens through which a topic can be seen; a hook is the specific entry point that makes a reader care at sentence one.

### Angles

- **The underexplained mechanism**: Take something readers accept as true and explain why it is true at a level of detail they have not seen before.
- **The useful contrarianism**: Challenge a common assumption — but only if you have a better model to offer, not just a critique.
- **The pattern across cases**: Show that a phenomenon the reader associates with one domain appears the same way across three or more different domains. The pattern is the insight.
- **The failure autopsy**: Examine something that went wrong and work backward to the decision or assumption that caused it.

### Hooks

- **The specific scene**: Drop the reader into a concrete moment before introducing the concept. Specificity creates belief.
- **The counterintuitive claim**: Open with a claim that contradicts an assumption most readers hold, then spend the article earning it.
- **The relatable frustration**: Name a frustration the reader has felt but not articulated — then position the piece as the resolution.
- **The stakes statement**: Tell the reader what is at risk if they do not understand what follows.
