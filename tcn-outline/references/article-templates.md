# The Civic Node Article Templates — Full Reference

*Four templates for structuring Substack articles. Each maps naturally to one of the five viral triggers. The template is scaffolding, not a cage — it solves the "what do I write and how do I structure it" problem without becoming a formula.*

---

## Template Selection Guide

| Template | Best for | Natural trigger | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triple Connection | News events with historical depth | Unexpected Connection | A current event has a precedent nobody is citing, and the future implications haven't been named |
| System Audit | Broken systems, institutional failures | Named Hypocrisy | Something is obviously wrong, and the real cause is structural, not incidental |
| Concept Decoder | Complex ideas the audience needs vocabulary for | Precision Gift | Marcus is circling an idea but lacks the framework to articulate it precisely |
| Pattern Report | Multiple signals pointing in the same direction | Early Warning | Several developments, separately unremarkable, reveal a structural shift when viewed together |

---

## Template 1: "The Triple Connection"

**Structure:** Current Event -> Historical Context -> Tech/Future Implications

**Section 1: The Spark** (400 words)
- What happened and why it matters right now
- Tone: direct, grounded in specifics — names, numbers, dates
- This section earns the reader's attention by being timely and specific
- End with the question the reader should be asking but isn't

**Section 2: The Pattern** (500 words)
- Historical precedent or deeper structural context
- This is where operational credibility shows — cite primary sources, not summaries
- The reader should feel like they're seeing a pattern they missed
- Tone: analytical, building — longer sentences are fine here because you're constructing an argument

**Section 3: The Protocol** (400 words)
- What this means for technology, decentralization, or the systems the reader operates in
- Connect back to the core domain: who controls power, and do individuals have recourse?
- Tone: forward-looking but grounded — implications, not predictions
- Land on a specific, actionable insight — not a vague "time will tell"

**Section 4: Personal Code** (200-300 words)
- Brief personal reflection that connects this to lived experience
- This is where Justin's voice is most direct — first person, less analytical, more human
- The personal material enters in service of the argument, not as memoir
- Can be vulnerable but not confessional — emotional exposure is fine, professional liability is not

**Total target:** 1,500-1,600 words

## Template 2: "The System Audit"

**Structure:** Problem -> Analysis -> Solutions

**Section 1: The Glitch** (400 words)
- Identify the systemic problem with surgical specificity
- Open with the most concrete manifestation — a specific event, number, or quote that makes the problem tangible
- Tone: sardonic precision — name what's broken without rage
- The reader should recognize the problem but not yet see why it exists

**Section 2: The Source Code** (500 words)
- Why this problem exists — the structural cause, not the surface explanation
- This is where the "charitable reading then audit" move lives: present the official explanation fairly, then show why it's insufficient
- Use evidence from multiple sources; cross-reference the wiki
- Tone: building analytical weight — this is the densest section

**Section 3: The Upgrade** (400 words)
- How the system could work differently — decentralization, technology, policy reform, or structural redesign
- Be specific about mechanisms, not vague about ideals
- Acknowledge trade-offs honestly — what this solution sacrifices and why it's still worth it
- Tone: measured optimism grounded in evidence, not cheerleading

**Section 4: My Debug** (200-300 words)
- Personal experience with this system — as a user, operator, or observer
- The anecdote should illustrate the argument, not distract from it
- This is where humor as pressure valve works best — heavy topics need the release
- End with a personal stake: why this matters to Justin specifically

**Total target:** 1,500-1,600 words

## Template 3: "The Concept Decoder"

**Structure:** Definition -> Mechanics -> Applications

**Section 1: The Definition** (300 words)
- What is this concept, in plain language?
- Open with the analogy that narrows: broad enough to hook many readers, specific enough to point toward the thesis
- Strip jargon — write for the intelligent-but-not-credentialed reader
- Tone: clear, accessible, slightly irreverent about unnecessary complexity

**Section 2: The Mechanics** (600 words)
- How it actually works — the part most explainers skip or hand-wave
- This is the longest section because it's doing the hardest work: making a complex mechanism legible
- Use concrete examples, not abstract descriptions
- Diagrams or structured comparisons where they genuinely help (not decoration)
- Tone: precise, patient — this is where operational credibility is earned
- Cite sources; footnotes build trust

**Section 3: The Applications** (400 words)
- Current and potential use cases — where this concept operates in the real world
- Connect to topics the reader already follows: monetary policy, DePIN, institutional behavior, power dynamics
- Name what's working and what isn't — no cheerleading
- Tone: forward-looking, analytical

**Section 4: The Human Element** (200-300 words)
- Why this matters beyond the technical — what it means for people
- Personal reflection: how Justin encountered this concept, what changed in his thinking
- The close should reframe the opener's analogy in light of what the piece established
- Leave the reader with a question worth investigating, not a conclusion they already drew

**Total target:** 1,500-1,600 words

## Template 4: "The Pattern Report"

**Structure:** Listicle — multiple signals organized around a thesis

This is the most flexible template. The number of items varies (4-7 is the sweet spot). Each item is a self-contained observation that supports the overarching pattern.

**Opener** (200 words)
- Name the pattern directly — what are these signals pointing toward?
- The thesis is the pattern itself, stated clearly

**Items** (200-300 words each)
- Each item: specific signal + evidence + what it means for the pattern
- Items should escalate in significance or surprise — don't front-load the strongest example
- Cross-link between items where connections exist
- Tone per item: analytical, specific, grounded in data

**Personal Reflection** (200 words)
- Mandatory — even in a listicle format
- What this pattern means for Justin personally, or how he spotted it
- Brief; don't overstay

**Close** (100-150 words)
- What the pattern implies going forward
- Callback to the opener
- End on the implication, not the instruction

**Total target:** 1,200-1,500 words depending on item count

## Universal Rules Across All Templates

**Section subheadlines:** Every section heading gets a short subheadline that previews the section's specific argument. Format: `## The Source Code: How a Non-Binding Letter Became a Binding Market`. Under 12 words, specific, slightly provocative. Someone scanning only the headers should get the argument's shape. The outline should propose these; the draft finalizes them.

**Openers:** Always an analogy that narrows from broad to specific. The lighthouse that attracts the ship. Engage from the first word; thesis lands by paragraph 2.

**Closers:** Callback to the intro analogy, restated in the light of what the piece established. The reader should come away pondering something or wanting to dig further. Never end with a conclusion the reader already drew from the setup — the close must add one more thing.

**Personal reflection:** Mandatory in every template. Personal material enters as illustration, not destination. The anecdote serves the argument. There are things that belong to Justin that don't belong to drinkYourOJ; the line is real.

**Citations:** Source claims with footnote links. Err toward more citations, not fewer. The citations are for the reader's trust, not just your confidence.

**Length:** 1,200-1,600 words. The ceiling is what fits comfortably in a Substack email. If the idea needs more room, it's a multi-part series — don't cram.

**Humor:** Present in every template as a pressure valve. Heavy topics especially need it. But the humor serves the argument; it's never decoration or distraction.
