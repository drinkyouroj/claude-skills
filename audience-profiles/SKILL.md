# Audience Profiles — The Civic Node

This skill applies the research-validated reader persona for The Civic Node to any content, marketing, offer, pricing, or product decision. Always load `references/marcus-profile.md` before making audience-based recommendations.

---

## When to use this skill

Load this skill and apply the Marcus profile whenever:

- **Writing** — evaluating article angles, hooks, openings, section depth, or tone fit
- **Marketing** — crafting subject lines, landing page copy, social media, or calls to action
- **Offers** — structuring free vs. paid tiers, deciding what belongs behind the paywall
- **Pricing** — setting or adjusting subscription price, annual vs. monthly framing
- **Products** — evaluating whether a new product, tool, or resource will resonate
- **Decisions** — any "should I do X" question where audience fit is the crux

## How to apply this skill

### Step 1 — Load the persona
Read `references/marcus-profile.md` in full before making any recommendation. This file contains:
- Full demographic and psychographic profile
- Day in the life
- Hopes, fears, emotional triggers, and decision dynamics
- Prior experience and biases
- Before/after transformation
- What makes the newsletter distinct in his mind

### Step 2 — Map the task to the right lens

| Task type | Primary lens to apply |
|---|---|
| Article angle or hook | Hopes & goals + emotional triggers |
| Opening paragraph | Day in the life (what's on his mind right now) |
| Depth and sourcing | Prior experiences and biases (he reads footnotes) |
| Paid tier or paywall | Decision dynamics + what slows subscription |
| Subject line or tweet | "I'd send this" test + conversion sentence pattern |
| Pricing | Competing priorities (time/attention ROI, not cost) |
| New product or tool | Before/after + job-to-be-done |
| Landing page copy | Fears + what makes this distinct |

### Step 3 — Apply the Marcus test

Before finalizing any recommendation, run it through these five questions:

1. **Signal test** — Does this give Marcus something he couldn't easily find himself?
2. **Shill test** — Does anything here feel like promotion dressed as analysis?
3. **Send test** — Would he forward this to someone? If not, why not?
4. **Jersey test** — Does this feel like a team jersey (tribal) or genuine analysis?
5. **90-second test** — If this asks him to pay or act, is the value case made before he loses the impulse?

### Step 4 — State your reasoning

When making a recommendation, briefly anchor it to a specific persona insight. Example:
> "This hook works because Marcus is already suspicious of confident takes with no sources — opening with the source upfront disarms that reflex immediately."

## Quick reference — core Marcus facts

**Who he is:** Solo operator, 32-42, crypto-native, runs 1-3 income streams including a DePIN node. Former professional context (fintech, tech). Politically literate, economically skeptical, allergic to oversimplification.

**What he reads for:** Sense-making under ambiguity. Synthesis he can't build himself. Vocabulary for positions he already holds intuitively but can't defend precisely.

**What makes him stay:** Consistent signal floor. Writer clearly operates in the thing they write about. Sardonic but rigorous. No tribal jersey. Footnotes that check out.

**What makes him leave:** Shill energy. Repetition without evolution. Moralizing. One factual error on something he knows well collapses credibility across everything else.

**What converts him to paid:** Accumulated proof over 3-4 issues. Not a single great issue — a consistent pattern of being slightly ahead of him. Upgrade happens in a 90-second window when impulse peaks.

**His internal "worth it" bar:** Two out of three issues deliver at least one thing he could not have found easily himself.

## Applying to specific use cases

### Writing an article
Load the full profile. Check: Is the hook something he'd encounter in his daily life or reading? Does the sourcing model his own research approach? Does it give him vocabulary or framework he can use? Does it end with implications, not conclusions he's already drawn?

### Evaluating a hook or subject line
Apply the "send test" directly: would Marcus forward this to one specific person he knows? If yes, it's a strong hook. If no, identify why — is it too tribal, too surface-level, too familiar, or too vague?

### Structuring paid vs. free tiers
Marcus upgrades on accumulated proof, not one great issue. The paid tier should consistently deliver the "framework behind the analysis" — not just more volume. Free issues should always leave one clear thing implied: there's a layer beneath this he's not seeing yet.

### Setting or defending a price
Marcus does not calculate monthly cost — he calculates clarity per hour of reading. Frame pricing around time saved and decision quality improved, not access to content. At $10-15/month, the conversion lever is not price; it's proof of value density.

### Writing landing page or about page copy
His two biggest objections on arrival: (1) Is this writer actually in the thing they're writing about? (2) Is this going to be one of those newsletters that sounds smart but says nothing? Address both directly. Credentials mean nothing. Operational history means everything.

### Evaluating a new product or tool
Map it to his before/after gap: does it help him move from "second-guessing his read" to "holding the position with precision"? If the product shortens his research loop or gives him a credible filter for evaluating projects, it passes. If it's content for content's sake, it won't convert.
