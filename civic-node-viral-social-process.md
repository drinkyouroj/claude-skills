# Viral Social Media Post Process — The Civic Node

*Synthesized from: Justin Hearn voice profile, Marcus reader persona, Thais Freitas viral content research, and platform-specific mechanics.*

---

## The Core Insight

Virality for The Civic Node is not a reach problem. It's a precision problem.

Marcus — the ideal reader — has one conversion trigger: *"I would have sent this to someone."* That impulse, felt by enough Marcuses simultaneously, is what a viral post looks like from your end. Everything in this process is designed to trigger that impulse reliably.

He doesn't share things that are broadly interesting. He shares things that are precisely right — posts that articulate what he was thinking but couldn't say, name something broken with surgical accuracy, or spot a structural shift before it's obvious. He shares things that reflect on his judgment, because what he recommends says something about who he is.

That's the entire brief. Don't write for reach. Write for the moment Marcus reads it and immediately opens a new tab to quote-tweet it.

---

## Why Most Civic Node Posts Don't Go Viral

The failure modes are predictable:

**The analysis is good but the hook is written for someone already interested.** Marcus never gets past the first line because the first line assumes he already cares. He doesn't. Yet.

**The take is correct but not surprising.** Marcus already suspected it. You confirmed what he thought, but you didn't get there from an angle he didn't expect. No gap to close; no forward motion.

**The post explains the joke.** The observation is made and then restated in case the reader missed it. Every word after the landing dilutes the landing.

**The emotion is diffuse.** The post is trying to be interesting rather than trying to be specifically useful or specifically funny or specifically alarming. Diffuse emotion doesn't trigger sharing; specific emotion does.

## The Five Viral Triggers for This Audience

Research on viral content (Thais Freitas, Jonah Berger's *Contagious*) consistently shows that people share what makes them feel something specific. For The Civic Node's audience, there are five emotions that drive sharing, in rough order of frequency and force:

**1. The Precision Gift** — Marcus was circling a thought for days. You close it in one sentence. The emotion is a mix of recognition, relief, and mild embarrassment that he didn't say it first. This is the most powerful trigger for the audience; it's the one that converts to paid subscribers, not just likes.

**2. The Early Warning** — You've named a structural shift before it's obvious. The emotion is urgency: fear of being late. Marcus shares it because he wants to be the person who spotted this early, and sharing it is the proof.

**3. The Named Hypocrisy** — Something obviously broken or dishonest gets named with dry precision. The emotion is validated frustration — the sardonic framing converts private irritation into public statement. He shares it as a way of saying "this is what I've been saying."

**4. The Devastating Compression** — A complex situation, reduced to one sentence that's accurate and brutal. Example: "In 2024, one side brought a policy paper to a meme war, and lost." The emotion is dark amusement; the shareability comes from the compression itself. People share great sentences.

**5. The Unexpected Connection** — Two things from different domains turn out to be the same problem at different scales. Monetary policy + DePIN token design. Regulatory capture + open-source governance. The emotion is surprise shading into "wait, obviously." Posts that generate this feeling spread because the reader wants others to feel it too.

**A note on negative emotions:** Anger and fear generate more comments than shares. They have their place — named hypocrisy and early warnings both carry negative charge — but pure rage-bait attracts the wrong people and is off-brand by policy. Don't optimize for comment count; optimize for the forward.

## The Five-Step Process

### Step 1: Mine for the Spark

Don't start by writing. Start by listening. Marcus is already having conversations in specific places — DePIN Discord servers, X crypto threads, macro finance corners, policy discussions. The spark is already there. Your job is to find it.

Specifically, look for:

- The take getting 3,000 likes that Marcus knows is directionally wrong but can't immediately refute with the specific numbers he needs. (Precision gift opportunity.)
- The debate where both sides are describing symptoms but nobody has named the underlying structure. (Naming opportunity.)
- A pattern that's been true for six months that just got publicly confirmed by an event. (Early warning, vindication variant.)
- A widely-shared claim that has a fatal flaw nobody is mentioning. (Named hypocrisy, if the flaw is structural rather than incidental.)
- Something from a domain Marcus cares about (DePIN economics, say) that maps exactly onto something from a domain he might not follow closely (Fed monetary mechanics, say). (Unexpected connection.)

The spark is almost never original. It's a real conversation already happening, where one precise observation is missing. Your job is to supply that observation, not to start the conversation.

### Step 2: Choose Your Emotional Architecture

Before writing a single word, decide which of the five triggers you're aiming for. This determines the structure, the tone, and the ending.

| Trigger | Tone | Structure | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| Precision gift | Dry, measured | Setup → precise close | The sentence. No elaboration. |
| Early warning | Understated urgency | Name the shift → implication | The implication, not the instruction |
| Named hypocrisy | Sardonic | Quote/fact → observation | Deadpan. |
| Devastating compression | Precise, brutal | One sentence or two | Done. |
| Unexpected connection | Curious, slightly amused | Domain A → Domain B → same thing | Let the reader complete it. |

Pick one. Posts that try to hit multiple triggers usually miss all of them.

### Step 3: Build the Hook

For Twitter/X, the hook is everything. The structure is the Benn Eifert model: quiet setup, borrowed text does the work, observation lands dry, never explain.

**The four hook structures that work for this voice:**

**A. The Borrowed Frame**
Quote, screenshot, or paraphrase something real — a headline, a tweet, an official statement. Let it sit. Follow it with one line of observation. Nothing else.

> *[Quote of the dumb take getting 3K likes]*
> "The number they're using was the starting point of a methodology they read backwards."

The gap between the quote and the observation is the content. Do not fill the gap.

**B. The Single Premise / Single Implication**
Two sentences. The first establishes something real. The second states the implication that nobody is saying.

> "The Fed has been describing the same structural problem differently for eight months depending on who's asking."
> "That's not communication. That's inventory management."

**C. The Compressed Narrative**
A complete situation rendered in one or two sentences, with the floor blowing out at the end. Modeled on: "Turns out I wasn't paranoid. I was just early."

> "In 2021, every DePIN project claimed decentralization. In 2024, most of them gave it a ticker symbol and called it done."

**D. The Rhetorical Question That Already Contains the Answer**
Used sparingly. The Socratic device — a question where the asking is also the indictment.

> "If a project needs to remind you it's decentralized, what does that tell you?"

**What never works:** hooks that start with "I've been thinking about..." or "Something interesting happened..." or "Have you ever wondered..." — these are performance of curiosity, not curiosity itself.

### Step 4: Execute and Stop

Write the post. Then stop earlier than feels comfortable.

The most common mistake is adding one more sentence to make sure the reader got it. If the reader didn't get it, the observation wasn't sharp enough — and adding a sentence to explain it confirms that. The fix is a sharper observation, not more words.

Rules at the execution stage:

- No em dashes in tweets. Spaced em dash only, and rarely.
- No AI hit-list phrases ("dive into," "it's important to note," "navigating the complexities of"). These signal low-effort immediately and will kill Marcus's trust before the second sentence.
- Profanity is a scarce resource. One use means zero elsewhere in the same post.
- Patterns of three get broken to two or four. The AI default.
- Parentheses are comedic whispers. Use them like that or not at all.
- Semicolons connect; em dashes interrupt. Choose accordingly.

The ending is not a call to action. Marcus does not respond well to "thoughts?" or "what do you think?" appended to an otherwise precise post. It undercuts the confidence that made the post worth reading. The ending is the last beat of the observation. Full stop.

### Step 5: Run the Five Marcus Tests

Before posting, apply these in order:

1. **Signal test** — Is there anything here Marcus couldn't have found himself in five minutes of searching? If not, you've confirmed something without adding anything.

2. **Shill test** — Does anything feel like promotion dressed as analysis? Check the language for tells: superlatives, unexamined enthusiasm, the word "revolutionary." If the answer is yes, find the flaw in whatever you're praising and lead with that instead.

3. **Send test** — Would Marcus forward this to one specific person he knows? (Not "some people." One specific person.) If yes, it's working. If no, identify why: too tribal, too surface-level, too familiar, too vague.

4. **Jersey test** — Does this feel like a team jersey (tribal affirmation) or genuine analysis? If it only resonates with people who already agree, it's not going viral — it's preaching to a choir that's already seated.

5. **90-second test** — If the post is designed to drive subscriptions or a paid upgrade, is the value case legible in the first read? Marcus's window between impulse and action is 90 seconds. If the post is purely analytical (not conversion-oriented), skip this test.

If it passes all five, post it. If it fails one, the fix is usually in Step 2 (wrong trigger chosen) or Step 4 (over-explained).

## Platform-Specific Calibration

### Twitter / X

Full compression. The Benn Eifert model. Borrowed text preferred where available — screenshots of the wrong take, official statements, price charts with dry observation attached. One to three parts maximum. Never explain the joke. The platform rewards confidence and punishes hedging; a post that qualifies itself into safety reads as low-conviction.

Thread format is appropriate for the Unexpected Connection trigger only — where the reader needs to be walked from Domain A to Domain B. Even then, each tweet in the thread should stand alone as an observation, not as a sentence fragment that only works in sequence.

### LinkedIn

Same voice underneath, one more sentence of scaffolding. Marcus on LinkedIn is in a slightly more professional register and will tolerate one additional line of context-setting before the observation. This is not permission to add a conclusion after the landing — it's permission to assume he needs slightly more runway before liftoff.

Never use the one-sentence-per-paragraph format that dominates LinkedIn. That's the borrowed-authority format Justin explicitly identifies as the worst kind of engagement farming. Use prose. Use actual sentences.

### Substack Notes

The middle ground — can carry more analytical weight than a tweet, less than a full post. Good vehicle for the Precision Gift and Unexpected Connection triggers because the reader has self-selected as someone who wants more than a sentence. Notes that work well here often function as compressed versions of full posts — the seed before the article — and should make the reader feel like they're seeing early access to an argument that will be fully constructed later.

## What Viral Looks Like at Different Scales

Not every post needs to break through to an audience that doesn't already follow you. The Civic Node is building a specific kind of reader — Marcus types who accumulate proof over 3-4 issues before converting to paid. "Viral within your audience" (forward rates, quote-tweets from credible accounts, DMs that say "I've been saying this for months") is worth more than broad reach that attracts the wrong people.

Ragebait generates reach. It does not generate Marcus. The audience it builds is not the audience that pays, that reads footnotes, that checks sources, or that recommends the newsletter in contexts where it reflects on their judgment. Optimize for the right kind of viral: the kind where someone with taste shares it because it reflects on their taste.

---

## The Litmus Test

Before finalizing any post, one question:

> *Does this sound like something Justin would say to a specific smart person he respects — or does it sound like something written for an algorithm?*

If it's the former, post it. If it's the latter, cut it until it's the former. Less content, more compression. The viral posts are usually shorter than the ones that almost worked.
