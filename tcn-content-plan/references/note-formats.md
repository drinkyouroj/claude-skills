# TCN Note Formats — Reference

Seven formats are active in the content plan. Each has a specific job. The format isn't just a label — it determines what the Note promises the reader and how it should be structured.

---

## 1. Framework Hand

**Job**: Deliver one specific analytical move the reader can use immediately. Not information — a lens. The reader should finish the Note knowing how to read a category of events differently.

**Word count**: 50–80 words

**Structure**: Name the category of event. Describe the analytical move (what to look for first, what to ask, what to discount). One concrete live example.

**The tell**: A good Framework Hand leaves the reader with a mental checklist they'll apply the next time they encounter this type of story. A Framework Hand that just presents data and draws no move is a Primary Source Drop wearing the wrong label.

**Example hook**: "When you see a Fed rate decision story, ask one question before you read past the headline: what is actually moving the inflation number?"

**When to use**: When there's a live event that most readers are misreading because they lack the right analytical frame. Works best when the frame is contrarian or unexpected.

**When NOT to use**: When the goal is to let data speak for itself (that's Primary Source Drop). When you're challenging a specific claim someone made (that's Contested Claim).

---

## 2. Primary Source Drop

**Job**: Let the document do the work. Present data from a specific primary source with minimal editorial framing. The source IS the content.

**Word count**: 40–60 words

**Structure**: Data point(s). Source citation (name it explicitly — not "a study found"). One sentence of context at most. Stop. Do not editorialize.

**The tell**: A good Primary Source Drop reads like a well-formatted citation. The reader's reaction should be "that's striking" — not "what does the author think about this?" If the Note has more editorial sentences than data sentences, it's drifted into Framework Hand territory.

**Example hook**: "US interconnection queue, late 2024: ~2,290 GW of proposed capacity. US installed generating capacity: ~1,322 GW. More waiting to connect than is currently running."

**When to use**: When the data is striking enough to stand alone. When a primary source has just released figures that the financial/political press hasn't picked up yet.

**When NOT to use**: When the reader needs context to understand why the data matters (add a Framework Hand that day instead, as a separate Note).

---

## 3. Contested Claim

**Job**: Present a claim that was publicly made and publicly falsified (or supported) by a specific document or data point. Two documents, one falsification.

**Word count**: 50–80 words

**Structure**: State the claim (attributed if possible — "economists said," "the consensus was"). Present the document/data that challenges it. Draw the line between them explicitly.

**The tell**: A good Contested Claim has two documents — the forecast and the receipt. If there's only one document, it's probably a Primary Source Drop or a Framework Hand. The word "said" (attributed) and the word "then" (sequence) should appear or be implied.

**Example hook**: "Going into April, the word from economists: tariff pass-through was over... April core: +0.4% for the month. Largest monthly print since January 2025. They said pass-through was over. April says it wasn't."

**When to use**: When a specific published prediction or consensus view has just been contradicted by data. Especially strong when the original claim was made by credible sources — the gap between credibility and accuracy is the editorial tension.

**When NOT to use**: When you're not sure the original claim was actually widely held. A Contested Claim with a strawman opponent reads as bad faith.

---

## 4. Operator Observation

**Job**: Describe what a real operator actually did — a specific move by a company, institution, or actor — and name the pattern it represents.

**Word count**: 50–70 words

**Structure**: Name the operator and the move (concrete, specific — who, what, where). Name the constraint they were responding to. Name the pattern: what does this move reveal about how the constraint is being navigated?

**The tell**: A good Operator Observation is not analysis of what operators should do — it's a dispatch from what one actually did. If the Note is hypothetical or general ("companies are doing X"), it has failed the format. The operator must be named and the move must be documented.

**Example hook**: "Microsoft took over the Stargate Abilene site. Existing 1.2 GW grid connection included. Crusoe is building on-site generation on the same campus to bypass the utility queue. One acquisition, one buildout, both solving the same constraint by different routes."

**When to use**: When a specific company or institution has made a move that reveals how a larger constraint is being navigated in practice. Strongest when the operational detail is unexpected or under-covered.

**When NOT to use**: When the move hasn't actually happened yet (that's forecast, not observation). When the pattern can't be named clearly — if the "so what" is vague, the Note will feel thin.

## 5. Stress Test

**Job**: Pressure-test a belief the audience holds confidently against a primary source that challenges it. The audience's prior belief is the premise; the data is the test.

**Word count**: 80–100 words (slightly longer — needs to state the belief, apply the test, and draw the result)

**Structure**: State the belief (as the audience would state it, not as a strawman). Introduce the data or analysis that challenges it. State what the test reveals about the belief's accuracy.

**The tell**: A good Stress Test respects the belief before challenging it. It should feel like a genuine test, not a gotcha. The data should be primary-source concrete — not "studies suggest" but "the Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 report projects."

**Example hook**: "The claim is that the AI buildout is constrained by chips. Here's what the construction data says: 12 GW of US data center capacity was announced for 2026. 4 are under construction. The delivery miss is named in the filings: transformers, switchgear, batteries, circuit breakers."

**When to use**: When the audience has a confident prior belief that fresh data contradicts. Especially valuable for technically sophisticated readers who respond to primary-source challenges to consensus.

**When NOT to use**: When the data is ambiguous enough that a reasonable person could read it either way — the Stress Test requires a clear result, not a nuanced one.

## 6. Article Tease

**Job**: Post-publish — pull one claim, quote, or moment from the flagship piece and point the curious toward the full analysis. Not a summary. Not "new piece is live."

**Word count**: 40–55 words

**Structure**: One sharp claim or moment from the piece, standing alone. Then: "Full piece: [link]" or "[link]" with minimal transition.

**The tell**: A good Article Tease makes the reader want to click without telling them what the piece concludes. If it summarizes the argument, it's replacing the piece rather than teasing it. The test: could a reader who only saw the tease still be surprised by the piece?

**When to use**: Only after the flagship article is live. Typically 3–5 PM on the publish day.

**When NOT to use**: Before the piece is live. As a summary of a piece the author hasn't written yet (that's a bridge Note or a Framework Hand seeding the topic).

---

## 7. Cross-Domain Connection

**Job**: Show two news items from completely different beats that are running the same underlying play. The synthesis — naming what the two stories share — is the editorial value.

**Word count**: 60–80 words

**Structure**: Introduce both stories briefly (one sentence each). Name the structural parallel explicitly. Draw the implication.

**The tell**: A good Cross-Domain Connection has genuine explanatory power — the parallel isn't superficial. The two stories should come from domains that don't usually talk to each other in financial/political coverage (e.g., shipping routes + monetary policy; grid infrastructure + trade policy).

**When to use**: When two stories in genuinely different domains reveal the same mechanism or constraint. Strongest when readers of each beat wouldn't have noticed the connection on their own.

**When NOT to use**: When the parallel is forced or the domains aren't actually separate. Don't manufacture connections — wait for the real ones.

---

## Format Selection Notes

- **Never repeat the same format combination** used in the prior 2 days
- **At least one Primary Source Drop** per 3-day window (keeps the content grounded in documents)
- **Article Tease** only on flagship publish days, post-publish (3–10 PM window)
- **Cross-Domain Connection** only when two genuinely parallel stories exist — don't manufacture it
- **Operator Observation** requires a named operator and a documented move — don't use when the move is hypothetical or the actor is unnamed
